Writing a one page growth plan you will actually use

Purpose, targets and a monthly review on a single sheet.

Growth · 5 min read

Most business plans end up in a drawer. A one page plan survives because it is short enough to read every month and specific enough to change what you do on Monday.

1. Say what the business is for

Write two sentences: who you serve and what problem you solve for them. For example, "We bake fresh bread for neighborhood families who want a reliable breakfast without a trip to the mall." This statement helps you decide what to sell, where to open and what to turn down.

2. Pick three targets for the year

Choose numbers you can measure, such as monthly sales, gross margin, repeat customer rate or on time delivery. Three is enough. More than that and nothing gets attention. For each, write where you are today and where you want to be in twelve months.

3. Choose the few moves that get you there

List no more than five initiatives. Each should name an owner, a start date and a result you can check. "Improve marketing" is too vague. "Launch a loyalty card in March and reach 300 members by June" can be tracked.

Test every initiative. If it succeeds, which target moves, and by how much? If you cannot answer, drop it or sharpen it.

4. Name the risks

Write down the three things most likely to derail the plan, such as a key supplier increasing prices, losing a major client or a staff member leaving. Next to each, note one thing you will do to reduce the risk.

5. Review monthly, revise quarterly

Set a 45 minute meeting on the same day each month. Look at the three targets, check each initiative, and ask what to stop, continue or add. Every quarter, adjust the plan based on what you learned. A plan that never changes is not being used.

Common mistakes

  • Setting targets without a baseline, so you cannot tell if you are improving.
  • Giving every initiative to the owner, who then becomes the bottleneck.
  • Treating the plan as a document instead of a monthly conversation.
