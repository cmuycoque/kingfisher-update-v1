Writing a one page growth plan you will actually use

Purpose, targets and a monthly review on a single sheet.

Growth · 8 min read

When Dwight Eisenhower was asked how he managed such enormous responsibilities, he is said to have answered that plans are worthless, but planning is everything. The line is often quoted and rarely understood. A plan is a document that ages the moment it is printed. Planning is the discipline of deciding, together and in advance, what matters, so that when the unexpected arrives you are not deciding from scratch.

Small business owners tend to fall into one of two camps. The first has no plan at all and runs entirely on instinct, which works until it doesn't. The second spends a weekend producing a forty page document, files it, and never opens it again. Both camps end up in the same place, reacting. There is a third option that is quieter and far more effective: one page, written in plain language, reviewed every month.

First, say what the business is for

Begin with a sentence or two on why the business exists and who it serves. It sounds soft, but it does hard work. It decides which opportunities you say no to. A bakery that exists to serve neighborhood families will not chase a hotel supply contract that demands night shifts and price cuts, however attractive the headline number.

When a decision feels hard, return to the sentence. If the choice fits, proceed. If not, you have your answer.

Second, choose three targets, not ten

A target should be a number with a date. "Grow sales" is a wish. "Raise monthly sales from ₱600,000 to ₱780,000 by December" is a target. Pick no more than three for the year. Each additional target divides attention, and divided attention is how good intentions die in February.

A useful trio for most small businesses is one number for growth, one for profitability and one for the customer. For example: monthly sales, gross margin and repeat customer rate. Together they tell you whether you are getting bigger, healthier and better loved.

Third, break the growth into its levers

Every sales number is the product of simple pieces: how many people see you, how many of them buy, how often they come back and how much they spend each time. Splitting the target across these pieces turns a vague ambition into something you can act on.

Suppose the owner above wants a thirty percent rise. She could not realistically achieve it by shouting louder. But a small gain at each step compounds. A few more inquiries converting, a slightly higher repeat rate and a modest basket increase multiply into the target, and each lever has a clear owner and a clear action.

Fourth, pick the few moves that matter

For each lever, choose one move for the quarter. Not five. One. A move is specific: "Start a Saturday pre order for office orders," "Add a loyalty stamp for the third visit," "Introduce a combo at the register." Write down who owns it, what it will cost and how you will know it worked.

Many plans fail not because the ideas are bad but because there are too many of them. A business can only absorb so much change at once, and your team is also busy serving customers.

A plan with ten priorities has none. A plan with three gets done.

Fifth, name what could go wrong

List the three biggest risks: a key supplier raises prices, a star employee leaves, a competitor opens next door. Beside each, write what you would do. This takes fifteen minutes and costs nothing, and it removes the paralysis that follows a surprise. You are not predicting the future. You are rehearsing your reaction.

Review monthly, revise quarterly

On the first Monday of each month, sit down for thirty minutes with the plan and the actual numbers. Ask three questions. Where are we against each target? What worked? What will we change next month? Once a quarter, step back further and ask whether the targets themselves still make sense.

The review is the heart of the system. Without it you have a document, and with it you have a habit.

Mistakes we see most often

  • Targets without a baseline. You cannot decide a goal is ambitious until you know where you stand today.
  • Plans owned by one person. If your manager has never seen it, it is not a plan, it is a private hope.
  • Ignoring cash. A growth target that needs more stock, more staff and more space has to be matched by a way to fund them.
  • Never revising. A plan that cannot change is brittle, and a market that changes will break it.

Eisenhower was right that the plan matters less than the planning. A single page, honestly kept, gives a small business something large companies often lack: the clarity to say what it is doing, what it is not doing, and why.
