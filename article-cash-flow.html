Why cash flow, not profit, decides whether you survive

A profitable month can still leave you unable to pay your suppliers.

Operations · 6 min read

Many owners track profit and stop there. Profit tells you whether the business makes money over time. Cash flow tells you whether you can pay for tomorrow. When the two drift apart, healthy looking businesses fail.

Profit is an opinion, cash is a fact

Profit is sales minus costs, counted when the sale or cost happens. Cash is what actually moves through your bank account and till. If you sell on credit to a corporate client with 45 day terms, that sale shows as profit today but the money arrives next month. Meanwhile your supplier wants payment in 15 days and your staff want payday on the 15th and 30th.

Where the gap comes from

  • Slow collections. Unpaid invoices are profit you cannot spend.
  • Stock bought ahead. Inventory is cash converted into shelves. Overbuying locks it up.
  • Fast growth. A bigger order means paying for materials and labor before the customer pays you.
  • Big one time costs. Equipment, renovation and deposits are paid in full but spread across years in your profit figures.

A quick test. Add up what you must pay in the next 30 days, then add up what you can reasonably expect to collect. If the second number is smaller, you have a cash problem even if the books show a profit.

Four habits that fix most problems

1. Build a 13 week cash forecast. A simple spreadsheet with weekly rows for expected cash in and cash out. Update it every Monday. It shows you a shortfall weeks before it arrives.

2. Shorten the time to get paid. Ask for deposits, invoice the same day, and follow up on day one after the due date, not day thirty. Offering a small discount for early payment is often cheaper than borrowing.

3. Stretch payables carefully. Negotiate longer terms with large suppliers, but do not damage relationships by paying late without telling them.

4. Keep a reserve. Aim for enough cash to cover your fixed costs for at least one month, and build toward three. A reserve turns a surprise into an inconvenience.

What to track every week

Start with four numbers: cash on hand, money owed to you, money you owe, and the next 30 days of committed payments. If you can read these in two minutes every Monday, you will make calmer decisions about hiring, buying and expanding.
