Why cash flow, not profit, decides whether you survive

A profitable month can still leave you unable to pay your suppliers.

Operations · 8 min read

Consider a bakeshop in Paranaque that had its best month ever. A call center down the road ordered three hundred pastry boxes for a company event, the owner hired two extra hands, and the books closed with a profit nobody in the family had seen before. Eleven days later she could not pay her flour supplier. The business was not failing. It was winning, and winning was exactly what emptied the till.

This story repeats itself across small businesses everywhere, and it confuses owners because it breaks the one rule they were taught: that profit is the scoreboard. Profit is a fine scoreboard for a season. It is a poor one for next Tuesday, because bills do not care what your income statement says. They care what is in the bank.

Two clocks running at different speeds

Every business runs on two clocks. The first is the accounting clock. It records a sale the moment you make it and a cost the moment you incur it. The second is the cash clock. It records money only when it moves.

When the two clocks tick together, as they do for a small stall that is paid on the spot and pays suppliers on the spot, nobody needs to think about the difference. The trouble starts the day you extend credit, buy ahead, or grow. Each of those pushes the cash clock behind the accounting clock, and the gap is paid for with your own money.

The bakeshop gave the call center forty five day terms. Flour, butter, boxes and the extra wages were due in the first two weeks. For about six weeks the shop was owed far more than it had in the bank, and the chart below shows what that looked like.

Look at the gap between the two lines around week five. On paper the business was ahead by forty thousand pesos. In reality it was ten thousand pesos short of paying its people. The shop was not unprofitable. It was simply paying for its growth in advance.

Why growth is the most dangerous season

There is an old saying among bankers that more businesses die from too much success than too little. It is closer to a pattern than a joke. A larger order means more ingredients, more labor and more delivery costs, all paid before the customer pays you. The faster the growth, the larger the cash hole. A business that doubles its sales without changing how it collects and pays can need double the working capital, and many owners do not notice until a payroll is missed.

Three other things quietly widen the gap.

  • Slow collections. An unpaid invoice is profit you cannot spend. Many owners are reluctant to chase it because the customer is also a friend or a regular.
  • Stock bought ahead. Inventory is cash that has changed costume. A bulk discount feels like a saving until you realize you pre paid three months of supply.
  • One time costs. A new oven or a renovation leaves your account in one payment but appears in your profit over many years.
Profit is what you earn. Cash is what you can use. Only one of them pays the salary.

The test every owner should run on Monday

You do not need software to know whether you are exposed. Write down what you must pay in the next thirty days: payroll, rent, supplier bills, loan payments, taxes. Then write down what you can honestly expect to collect in the same period, counting only customers who have a real due date inside it. If the second number is smaller, you have a cash problem regardless of what the profit report says.

A rule of thumb. Aim to hold enough cash to cover one month of fixed costs, and build toward three. Reserves do not make a business richer. They make it harder to scare.

What the bakeshop changed

The owner did not borrow, and she did not stop taking big orders. She changed four small things, none of which needed an accountant.

She started a thirteen week forecast. One sheet, one row per week, with expected cash in and cash out. She updates it every Monday for ten minutes. The forecast would have shown her the shortfall three weeks before it arrived. Thirteen weeks is long enough to see trouble and short enough to be believable.

She asked for deposits. Fifty percent upfront on any corporate order above a set size, with the balance due at delivery. Customers rarely object. A company that can order three hundred boxes can pay a deposit, and the request signals that she runs a serious operation.

She invoiced the same day and followed up early. The reminder goes out the day after the due date, not thirty days later. Early reminders are friendly. Late ones are awkward for everyone.

She renegotiated with her largest supplier. Fifteen day terms became thirty, in exchange for a standing weekly order. She did not stretch payments silently. She asked, and she kept her word.

The second chart is not magic. It shows the same order and the same customer, with the only change being when the money moved. The lowest point of the cash line no longer drops below zero.

The four numbers to read every week

Resist the urge to build a dashboard. Start with four figures and read them every Monday: cash on hand, money owed to you and when it is due, money you owe and when it is due, and the next thirty days of committed payments. If you can see these in two minutes, you will make calmer decisions about hiring, buying and expanding, because you will know what the business can actually afford.

Profit tells you whether your model works. Cash tells you whether your model can survive its own success. A healthy small business watches both, but when forced to choose, it watches the cash.
