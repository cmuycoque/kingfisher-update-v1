How to cost a menu so every dish earns its place

Know what each plate really costs before you decide what to charge.

Operations · 8 min read

Restaurant consultants noticed something odd long ago. The dishes that diners praised most were not always the dishes that kept the lights on. A famous pasta could be the reason a place had a line out the door and also the reason it lost money on every plate. The kitchen was busy, the dining room was full, and the owner was still wondering where the profit went.

The gap between a dish people love and a dish that earns its keep is the gap that menu costing exists to close. It is not glamorous work. It involves a scale, a notebook and some patience. But it is the single most reliable way a food business can find money it already has.

Step one: write the recipe down

Most small kitchens run on memory. The cook knows how much garlic goes in, and the portion changes slightly with the mood and the shift. That flexibility is charming and expensive. A standard recipe fixes the quantity of every ingredient and the size of every portion. Without it, your costs are a moving target and your customers get a slightly different dish each visit.

Step two: price what you actually use

A kilo of chicken is not a kilo of usable chicken. Bones, trimming and cooking loss mean you may serve seventy percent of what you buy. Divide the price by the usable share to get the real cost per kilo. Do the same for vegetables, fish and anything that has a peel or a shell. Skipping this step is the most common reason menu costs come out too low.

An example. Chicken at ₱180 a kilo with seventy percent yield really costs about ₱257 per usable kilo. A recipe that uses 200 grams carries ₱51 of chicken, not the ₱36 that the receipt suggests.

Step three: find the food cost percentage

Add the cost of every ingredient in a single portion, including oil, sauce, garnish and the packaging for takeaway. Divide by the selling price. This is the food cost percentage. For many casual restaurants, a healthy range is roughly twenty five to thirty five percent, though it varies by concept. Treat the number as a question, not a verdict.

The chart shows a composite café menu. The adobo bowl and the iced coffee are comfortably inside the range. The salad and the pasta are not. The salad is a customer favorite, which is exactly why it is a problem: the better it sells, the more it costs the business.

Step four: sort every dish into one of four groups

Cost alone is not enough. A dish that costs a lot and sells a lot is a different problem from one that costs a lot and sells nothing. Plot each dish by how popular it is and how much it earns per plate.

  • Stars. Popular and profitable. Protect them, feature them and never let quality slip.
  • Workhorses. Popular but thin on margin. Adjust the portion, swap a costly ingredient or raise the price slightly.
  • Puzzles. Profitable but rarely ordered. Improve the name, the photo or the position on the menu, or ask the staff to recommend them.
  • Dogs. Neither popular nor profitable. Remove them, and enjoy the simpler kitchen.
A menu is not a list of what you can cook. It is a list of what you want to be asked for.

The hidden costs that ambush you

Ingredients are only part of the picture. Gas, electricity, labor, delivery commissions and spoilage all sit on top. A delivery app that takes a quarter of every order can turn a thirty percent food cost into a loss. If you sell through apps, calculate a separate price for them, or accept that you are paying for reach.

Waste deserves its own line. Ask the kitchen to record what is thrown away each day for two weeks. The totals are usually a surprise, and the fix is often as simple as smaller prep batches.

Make it a routine, not a project

Supplier prices move, and a menu that was profitable in January may not be in July. Recheck your five bestselling dishes every month and the rest every quarter. When a price shifts by more than five percent, update the cost sheet. When a dish falls outside your range, decide whether to adjust the recipe, the price or the plan.

The owner who knows what each plate costs is not a stingier cook. They are a freer one, because they know exactly which dishes they can be generous with and which ones are quietly paying for everything else.
